The Shore of Universes

Not every universe is allowed to exist. Some of them break the moment you try to build one — the maths falls apart in your hands.

Our job is to find the exact line between the universes that work and the universes that don't. We found part of that line. It looks like a coastline, so that is what we call it.

Scroll down. The shape in the window above will build itself as you go.

For grown-ups. Subject: the graviton scattering amplitudes of Cheung, Hillman and Remmen. The horizontal dial is the family parameter λ; the vertical axis is the spacetime dimension D. Everything drawn here is computed from the project's own exact engine, not sketched.

Chapter one

Every rule is a mountain

Physics hands us a whole family of rules. Each one says: your universe must stay underneath me.

Draw them all together and you get a mountain range. There are infinitely many peaks, marching off past the edge of the window.

Here is the thing about staying under all the mountains at once: only the lowest one is actually doing any work. The tall ones don't matter. You just have to duck under the valley floor.

For grown-ups. The surface is Tₖ(λ) = 3(2k−3)/(k(k−2)) · (λ² + (2k−2)λ + 1) + 2k, one sheet per integer spin level k ≥ 3, sampled on a 73×73 grid. Depth into the screen is k. The plateau at the back is a display clamp, not a feature of the physics — the range really does run off to infinity there.

Chapter two

The valley floor is the shore

That gold line is the lowest point of the whole mountain range, at every setting of the dial. It is the highest a universe can reach and still survive.

Watch it closely and you'll see it is made of pieces. As you turn the dial, a different mountain becomes the lowest one, and the line hands over from one to the next. We counted 18 handovers in this window. Each kink in the gold line is one mountain giving way to another.

For grown-ups. The shore is T̂(λ) = min over integers k ≥ 3 of Tₖ(λ), computed exactly at 241 values of λ. The kinks are the level handovers; proving where they sit needed a separate certificate of its own, because a minimum over infinitely many curves is not something you can check by looking.

Chapter three

Under the line is the sea

Everything below the gold line is the safe part: universes that hold together. That's the green sea, and it is what we are really trying to map.

Above the line is the dry, broken part. A universe up there doesn't just look wrong, it is impossible — it would need a probability somewhere to come out negative, and nature doesn't do that.

For grown-ups. The sea is the region D ≤ T̂(λ). Above it, unitarity fails: some partial-wave coefficient goes negative.

Chapter four

We test it by throwing knives

How do you find out whether a particular universe is fine? You throw a knife at it. A knife is a number you can compute about that universe. If it comes out plus, the universe survives. If it comes out minus, that universe is dead.

Every dot in the window is one universe with one knife thrown at it. Green means it lived. Red means it broke.

Now look where the red dots are. All of them are above the gold line. Not one red dot is under the water. We checked every dot with exact arithmetic — no rounding anywhere, no nearly-zero, no maybe.

For grown-ups. A knife is a signed coefficient of the amplitude in its Jacobi expansion, knife₁ = (−1)ᵐ fᵐ, computed by the same exact reference engine the project's certificates use. The grid is 41×41 in (λ, D), at knife orders j = 4 and j = 5 with n = 12. Signs are exact rationals; the count of negative knives below the shore is a self-check, and it is zero.

Chapter five

The part that isn't finished

Here is the problem, and it is the whole reason this is hard.

There isn't one knife. There are infinitely many — a new one at every depth, forever. In the window they're the walls fading away into the dark. We can throw the first few. A computer can throw a few thousand. But infinity is not a number of things you can check one at a time.

So checking isn't enough. We need a proof: one single argument that covers every knife at once, including the ones nobody will ever compute. We have proved a handful of the walls completely, and this year we found an argument that covers an infinite corner of the rest. The middle is still open.

That's what we're doing. Not knowing yet is the job.

For grown-ups. Each ghost wall is one depth. Depths 2 through 6 are certified by exact Bernstein positivity certificates with zero open boxes; depth 7 is banked but compute-bound. The keystone theorem — all depths by one argument — is open. The current front: a moment representation of the knife sum, a strict-total-positivity theorem for its kernel, and an asymptotic corner that does cover every depth at once, in an unbounded but incomplete region.
